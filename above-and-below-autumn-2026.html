EDITOR’S LETTER

A region worth staying for

Autumn 2026 · Above & Below · The Thames Club

Autumn changes the scale of attention. The long view across the water gives way to the nearer questions: what will keep the lights on, what will the winter cost, and who will still be here when spring returns? In southeastern Connecticut, those questions belong in the same conversation.

This edition follows three energy stories at different stages. Revolution Wind has reached commercial operation, but its legal story has not simply disappeared. Connecticut is weighing purchases of zero-carbon electricity while exploring a separate path toward advanced nuclear development. Across New England, winter preparation turns forecasts and generating capacity into a more immediate concern: whether energy can be delivered when it is needed.

Beyond the infrastructure lies another system, assembled one household at a time. A person can accept a job here without yet knowing how to make a life here. Housing, transportation, training, friendships and ordinary welcome determine whether opportunity becomes permanence. The newcomer might be arriving from another country, another state, military service or a different occupation. A longtime resident entering a new trade faces some of the same practical tests.

Above & Below looks at the visible announcement and the less visible work beneath it. A turbine turning offshore is an achievement. So is a trainee completing a course because transportation and childcare were possible. So is an unfamiliar face becoming a familiar one across a dinner table.

Our question this autumn is straightforward: how do we turn investment into a region that works, and a place people choose to call home?

ABOVE THE WATERLINE · ENERGY

Revolution Wind: power on, questions before the court

Commercial operation is a milestone. The remaining legal questions deserve their own account.

On November 5, Connecticut announced that Revolution Wind had reached commercial operation. The 704-megawatt offshore project has a contracted allocation of 304 megawatts for Connecticut and 400 for Rhode Island. That moves the story beyond construction progress to an operating source of electricity for the regional grid. 1

Its route to operation was interrupted by federal action. Rhode Island’s attorney general records the September 2025 lawsuit brought with Connecticut against the August 22, 2025 stop-work order, followed by further proceedings over a December suspension. The office lists a January 12, 2026 preliminary injunction protecting the project. These were challenges to government actions affecting construction; they should not be confused with a final resolution of every dispute concerning the project. 2

A separate proceeding continued into the autumn. CT Mirror reported in October that the Interior Department had asked the court for permission to rescind the project’s construction-and-operations-plan approval and reconsider it. That request arose in litigation brought by Green Oceans challenging federal approvals. Connecticut Public’s November 5 report said the request remained pending before U.S. District Judge Royce C. Lamberth. 3, 4

The distinction matters. An agency’s request to revisit an approval is not itself a court order withdrawing that approval. Equally, reaching commercial operation does not establish that litigation has ended. Readers deserve both facts without either being used to erase the other.

There is a similar distinction between a project’s rated capacity and the electricity it produces over a season. Megawatts describe a rate of output; megawatt-hours describe energy delivered over time. A wind farm’s contribution varies with conditions. Its winter value must be assessed alongside the rest of the system, rather than treated as a promise that one source can meet every hour’s demand.

For New London, the next chapter is about the durability of the work as well as the machinery offshore. The regional value of a major project is best judged over time: reliable operation, useful skills, sound contracts and a waterfront capable of serving future work. Commercial operation deserves recognition. Clear reporting on the unresolved proceedings remains part of that recognition.

BELOW THE HEADLINES · POLICY

Connecticut’s two nuclear conversations

Buying electricity and preparing for possible new reactors are different decisions.

Connecticut’s nuclear discussion has two tracks. One concerns the terms on which electricity is purchased. The other concerns whether, where and how a future generating facility might be developed. Combining them into a single announcement would obscure the decisions still ahead.

DEEP’s January 30 zero-carbon solicitation invited proposals from nuclear and other eligible resources. The agency said Connecticut’s existing Millstone and Seabrook contracts expire in 2029. It described the procurement as an opportunity to seek more affordable and stable rates, including through regional cooperation. Connecticut law requires participation by at least two other states in sharing costs for a new Connecticut procurement of existing nuclear generation. 5

There has been an autumn development, but it is not a nuclear award. On September 24, DEEP announced a multistate selection of 102.4 megawatts of new solar generation, including 63.4 megawatts for Connecticut. The same announcement said nuclear proposals were still being evaluated and would be addressed later. A selection announcement for nuclear power would be a distinct next step. 6

The second track is preparation for advanced nuclear technologies. In its August account of regional nuclear planning, Connecticut described a municipal opt-in pathway under Public Act 25-173 and a site-readiness program backed by $5 million in bond authorizations. It also described Public Act 26-46’s direction to study workforce needs and educational pathways. The announced regional summit was scheduled for September 30 in Lowell, Massachusetts. 7

Those steps establish a planning agenda. They do not demonstrate that a particular reactor has been licensed, financed or ordered. A useful public discussion will keep asking who carries construction risk, how costs would reach customers, what regulatory milestones apply, and what meaningful local participation requires.

Our region brings experience with nuclear work to this discussion. Experience is an asset; it is also a reason to ask precise questions. The strongest case for any investment should survive examination of cost, schedule, safety, workforce and alternatives. For readers following the next announcement, the first question should be: is this a proposal, a selection, an executed contract, a license or an operating plant? Each represents a different level of commitment.

REGIONAL NOTEBOOK · WINTER

Winter readiness lives in the details

A system can have enough generating capacity and still need close attention to fuel.

Winter preparation is often described as a forecast. It is better understood as a continuing operation. Weather changes, generators become unavailable, fuel inventories move, and a cold spell can last longer than expected. Readiness depends on what can be supplied repeatedly, not only on what can be started once.

ISO New England’s October 27 generator-readiness seminar said capacity was expected to be adequate for winter 2026–27. It also emphasized fuel replenishment under extreme conditions, monitoring of regional inventories, communication with generating resources and the role of its 21-day energy assessment in identifying potential constraints. The presentation warned that extreme cold can occur even when the seasonal outlook favors warmer conditions. 8

That assessment supports preparation, rather than complacency or alarm. Capacity measures the system’s ability to produce power at a given time. Energy adequacy asks whether it can sustain delivery through the conditions that actually occur. For readers, the practical lesson is to follow updated operational assessments rather than treating an autumn outlook as a guarantee for every winter day.

At home, affordability is another part of readiness. Connecticut is accepting applications for the 2026–27 Connecticut Energy Assistance Program. The Department of Social Services provides current eligibility and application information through its heating-assistance pages. In October, the governor also announced $12.7 million in supplemental state support for CEAP. Households should check the program’s current requirements rather than rely on last winter’s benefit amounts or deadlines. 9, 10

A community’s preparation includes making that information easy to find. An employer can share a clear assistance link. A landlord can communicate early about a heating problem. A neighbor can notice when someone has become isolated.

The energy system is regional, but the experience of winter is personal. A household does not encounter a reserve margin in the abstract; it encounters a room that stays warm and a bill it can manage. Good planning should keep both scales in view.

PEOPLE & PROSPERITY · ANALYSIS

The work of becoming established

A job offer begins a relationship with a region. Everyday arrangements determine whether it lasts.

A newcomer’s first useful map rarely looks like an economic-development map. It shows the route to work, a possible apartment, a grocery store, a school office and the person who can answer the next question. Success depends on how those points connect.

Eastern Connecticut already has an example of connecting preparation to employment. The Eastern Connecticut Workforce Investment Board’s Manufacturing Pipeline Initiative offers no-cost training designed around hiring needs at Electric Boat and other manufacturers. The program also identifies financial support intended to reduce barriers to training and employment. CT State’s account of its participation describes assistance that can include travel reimbursement, daycare assistance and required books, tools and clothing. Availability and individual arrangements should be confirmed with the program. 11, 12

The important lesson reaches beyond manufacturing. A course can be free while participation remains expensive. Travel, lost working hours and care responsibilities can prevent someone from reaching the classroom. A training strategy becomes more useful when it takes those costs seriously.

The same logic applies after hiring. A household needs a workable relationship between wages, rent and the journey to work. The Southeastern Connecticut Council of Governments describes affordable and diverse housing as necessary to regional vitality and supports municipalities through studies, training and planning assistance. That is a framework for action, not evidence that the region’s housing needs have already been met. 13

Newcomers also need information that residents have stopped noticing they possess. Which office handles this form? How does one find a reliable service provider? Where can a spouse or partner meet people outside the workplace? An answer offered early can save time and make an unfamiliar place less intimidating.

For employers and civic organizations, a useful measure of welcome would be continuity. Is there someone to ask after the first week? Can a worker identify a next step in training? Does a family know where to turn when an arrangement falls through? Recruitment attracts attention to the entrance. Retention asks whether the path beyond it is navigable.

Economic opportunity becomes stronger when the practical work of settling in receives the same seriousness as the announcement of new jobs.

PLACES & BELONGING · ESSAY

What makes a community worth remaining in?

The answer is built through repeated encounters, useful institutions and room for a future.

A community worth remaining in offers more than a successful first year. It offers the possibility of a second stage: a better job, a different home, new friendships, a place for children to grow, or a way to remain involved after retirement. The question is whether people can change their lives without having to leave the region to do it.

That possibility has a material foundation. Housing must serve more than one household type. Training must offer more than an entry point. Transportation must connect the places people can afford with the places where they work. A pleasant waterfront cannot compensate for every daily arrangement that fails.

Yet arrangements alone do not create attachment. Belonging grows through repetition: someone remembers a name; a familiar place expects a return; a conversation continues beyond an introduction. Libraries, neighborhood businesses, arts groups, volunteer organizations, faith communities and social clubs can all provide those repeated encounters.

The Thames Club can consider its own part in that work. A guest introduction, a shared table, a concert discussion or a bowling evening can make the region more legible to someone who has not yet found a circle of acquaintances. Such encounters give members a practical way to offer welcome.

An old institution has something useful to offer when it makes continuity available to people who do not share its history. The point is not to require a newcomer to become an expert on local tradition before feeling comfortable. It is to make participation possible, then let familiarity grow.

Nor should growth be measured only by how well it attracts people from elsewhere. A young resident taking a first skilled job, a parent returning to employment and an older neighbor seeking a smaller home also need a place in the region’s future. A community that welcomes newcomers while overlooking those already here has left part of the work undone.

The autumn test is therefore larger than a count of projects or hires. Can a person find useful work, a workable home and relationships that survive a change in circumstances? Energy investments help sustain the economy. Housing and training help people enter it. Community gives them a reason to imagine staying.

When winter comes, a region’s strength is felt in ordinary continuities: the work proceeds, the home remains warm, and there is somewhere to go where one is recognized. Those are modest ambitions in language, substantial ones in practice.
